# Urgency Review

Allen: "Can you look at my vault for Building urgency in the game. I have some loose thoughts on how I felt from when I was playing "Create Pottery" and what I thought made me want to stay around for longer. I'd like to organize these thoughts, analyze why their game works psychologically, and finally understand how to apply it to our game."

Sources: Allen's notes (original wording: [[Archive/Design/Building urgency — original thoughts — 2026-10-23]]), [[Design/Pottery Tutorial Review]] for what the recording shows, and the design and build records through [[Sessions/2026-10-23#More refinements]] (cart at 0.8×, rickshaw bays, the crafter claiming stations). Create Pottery! details are Allen's account unless the recording shows them. The psychology below is well-studied effects applied to one player's experience: design hypotheses, not measured causes. Nothing was played or changed in Studio. Proposals are not decisions until Allen picks them.

[[Building urgency in the game]] holds a second organized version of the same notes, rewritten in place the same afternoon outside this session; this note leaves it as it is.

## The short version

- **Create Pottery! always has a next job, and being slow only ever costs a missed gain.** Its workshop is a chain of stations joined by shelves; something is always filling, emptying or finishing, and the player chooses what to tend. Nothing Allen made was taken away.
- **It layers every kind of pull:** waits that overlap with work, idle workers and empty shelves that read as losses, side goals on their own clocks, surprises at random times (rushes, VIPs), offers you have to walk over to see, and hires that take one job at a time off your hands.
- **Sell Swords! already has most of the machinery:** a furnace that heats while you forge, a shop that needs stock, mail orders every 3 minutes, the cart, a crafter with a rack in and a rack out. **What's missing is signalling and surprise.** The game has one sound, an empty shop or a stopped crafter says nothing, every customer is alike, and nothing random ever happens.
- **Suggested order:** a sound and a sight for every station (P1) and offers you can win (P2) → VIP buyers (P3) → after the playtest, a spare station for the player and the next hires (P4, P5) → sunny spells and lucky hours once production can answer them (P6, P7) → an empty shop that shows (P8).

## 1. Allen's notes, organized

"Players should feel a sense of urgency … it always felt like there's something more I could be doing."

| **The fantasy** | "It should feel like the player is operating a large business, because they are." |

## 2. Why it works

**The model: a chain of stations joined by shelves, with the player as the scheduler.** Each station makes work for the next or uses it up; each shelf between them is filling or emptying; each timer is counting down. At almost any moment one of them wants attention, and the player decides which. Each reason below is one way that chain pulls.

### Waits that overlap with work

The kiln fires while the next pot is shaped, so the wait is never empty and the walk back pays off: the pot is done. When the two take about as long, they lock into a rhythm. People tend to go back to tasks they left unfinished (Ovsiankina, 1928; popularly the "Zeigarnik effect"), and a kiln holding something you made is an unfinished task you know you'll close. What you made yourself also counts for more (the IKEA effect: Norton, Mochon & Ariely, 2012); Pottery's long wait comes after the work, on the pot.
**Needs:** a wait about as long as the next task.

### Idle and empty read as losses

An idle worker or an empty shelf is money you can see yourself not making. Losses weigh roughly twice as much as equal gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), and once a worker is yours, working is the normal you measure against, so a stopped worker feels like a loss. Hence "I need to immediately roll more clay." Nothing is actually taken, which keeps it motivating rather than punishing.
**Needs:** visibility. A stopped worker you can't see or hear costs nothing.

### Something might be happening

Rewards come on four kinds of schedule, and Pottery runs all four: every sale pays (fixed ratio); clay rolls with rarities (variable ratio); the kiln and the van run on clocks (fixed interval); customers, rushes and VIPs come when they come (variable interval). The last produces steady, unprompted checking (Ferster & Skinner, 1957), which is Allen's "check the shop more."
**Needs:** checking must sometimes find something worth it. If every customer is the same, there's nothing to check for.

### The odd one out gets noticed

A highlighted VIP is the one thing glowing in the room, and the distinct item is the one noticed and remembered (the isolation effect: von Restorff, 1933). Rare, valuable and unannounced, a VIP is a small jackpot. A rush or a luck boost makes the next few minutes worth more than usual, and a closing window makes now matter (scarcity).
**Needs:** a way to answer: stock to sell in a rush, room to roll in a lucky spell. Otherwise the window only shows what you can't do.

### The shop is a second place that needs you

Offers you can only see at the customer split the business into two places, workshop and shop, that both want the player, and walking between them is the job. Missing one costs a sale, never anything owned.
**Needs:** a window you can win: long enough to finish the current action and walk over. The pottery tutorial's offer ran 60 seconds ([[Design/Pottery Tutorial Review]]).

### Relief you've earned, one job at a time

Having done a job by hand, you know exactly what a hire saves, so the hire feels big, and a visible goal speeds you up as you near it (the goal-gradient effect: Hull, 1932; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006). Each hire takes one job, and its output becomes your next job: the potter makes pots for Allen to fire. The pull doesn't go away; it moves up a level, from making pots to feeding and emptying the workers. The spare station means the player is never locked out and never just watching.

New jobs (deliveries, requests, VIPs) arrive about as fast as old ones are automated, so the player stays busy without being swamped. Challenge matched to skill is the condition for flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). That's how it feels like a large business without feeling like more work.

### Choosing is the game

Nothing tells Allen the order: fire or shape, stock or sell. Real choices (autonomy), visible results of good choices (competence) and people to serve (relatedness) are what self-determination research finds behind enjoying games (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski, 2006).

### What keeps it from being stressful

In Allen's account the crafts themselves aren't timed, every cost of being slow is a missed gain, and the player picks which plate to let wobble. That's the line for Sell Swords!: **urgency between crafts, calm within them; you can miss gains, but never lose what you have.**

## 3. Sell Swords! today

| Pull | Create Pottery! | Sell Swords! (recorded through 2026-10-23) | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overlapping work | Kiln fires while you shape | The furnace heats the next ingot (10 s) while you forge (12 strikes, about 9 s at the fastest): the same rhythm | Nothing teaches it; "Ready!" is silent |
| Shop stock | Empty shelves leave customers nothing to buy | Customers only come while a sword is on display | An empty shop is just quiet: the loss is invisible |
| Worker supply | The clay shelf feeds the potter | The Ingot Rack (12) feeds the crafter, which fills the Finished Sword Rack (6) | A stopped crafter just stands at Anvil 2 (no bubbles, Allen's choice); nothing reaches you in the shop |
| Side goals | Phone requests, van orders | Mail: letters and an order every 3 min (amber dot); the cart: bulk at 0.8×, 6–15 swords, 70–150 s trips | In place |
| Earned relief | Potter, then kiln operator; a spare station | The crafter makes whole swords at Anvil 2, and your Anvil 1 stays free. Since More refinements it claims the furnace, rack or stone it's heading to | With one furnace, the crafter can lock you out of heating |
| Surprises | Rushes, luck boosts, VIPs | Walk-ins every 12–22 s, all alike; weather traffic and special visitors designed, not built | Nothing worth checking for |
| Being there | Offer seen near the customer, 60 s | Bubble within 35 studs, 20 s, silent | Too short to finish a forge; no cue |
| Cues | Not assessed (the recording's audio wasn't reviewed) | One sound in the place, the anvil's `Clang` | No ding, bell, chime or coins |

Of the four schedules, Sell Swords! runs three in full: sales pay (fixed ratio), the loot box (variable ratio), the furnace, mail clock, cart and daily reward (fixed interval). The fourth exists only in its weakest form: walk-ins come at random, but they're all alike. **The plates are already spinning; the game doesn't tell you when one wobbles, and none of them ever surprises you.**

**When each pull matters.** Customers currently outnumber swords: about 3 walk-ins a minute against 1–1.5 swords ([[Design/Alignment Review — 2026-10-23#Balance findings|Alignment finding 5]], estimates). A customer rush does nothing for a player still making every sword by hand. What limits income moves as the workshop grows, and each stage has its own urgent feeling:

| Stage | Limits income | Feels urgent | Relief you buy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hands (before the crafter) | You, at one anvil | Furnace loaded, a sword on the shelf, the customer served | Blueprints, Faster Heating, Furnace 2 |
| Feeding (crafter hired) | Ore in, swords out | Ingot Rack empty, Finished Sword Rack full, shop empty | Ore Slots, Lucky Roller, rack space, the stocker |
| Customers (stocker, better crafters) | Walk-ins, and being there for offers | Offers waiting, rushes | Better Advertising, More Clients, Rickshaw Bays, Upgraded Store, the cashier |
| Prestige (cashier, ore attendant) | Special buyers | VIPs, lucky hours, new traditions | Reputation, blueprints, the next tradition |

This is Allen's own arc in Pottery (everything by hand, then the potter, then the kiln operator), and it sets when each proposal belongs.

## 4. Proposals

### P1. A sound and a sight for every station, no text

Every open loop gets a cue that reaches across the plot:

- **Furnace:** a soft ding and a brighter glow when the ingot is ready.
- **Shop:** a door chime when a customer walks in; coins when a sale lands.
- **Offer:** one short cue when a customer starts an offer.
- **Crafter:** its hammering heard from the shop door, so silence means it has stopped. Stopped, it waits by the empty rack with folded arms, and the crew button gets the amber dot (the Mail button's dot, reused).
- **Cart:** wheels and a bell as the courier backs in.
- **Mail:** a paper rustle when an order arrives.

Why: a loop only pulls if you notice it, and sound carries without the labels Allen keeps out of the UI. The `Effects` sound group and the Sound switch already exist. Keep each sound short and quiet, one per event, never repeating.

### P2. Offers you can win

Keep what Allen liked: the price is only seen at the customer. Lengthen the offer from 20 s to about 45 s (tuning; Pottery's was 60), with P1's offer cue. Don't pause offers while you craft, as [[Design/Core Loop Review#Proposed refinement 6: remove interruptions and preserve active crafting|Core Loop refinement 6]] floated: pausing removes the pull, while a longer window keeps it and removes the unfair misses. This answers the Alignment Review's "offers during crafting" item.

### P3. VIP buyers

A rare walk-in, highlighted as they step in (a gold rim light and their own sound), who wants one thing shown as an icon over their head: an etched blade, a Rare+ metal, a polished sword, a katana. They pay well over value for it (start around 1.5×; a liked regular sale is about 1.25×), plus gems and reputation, and wait longer than a regular (about a minute) before leaving. Only the player serves them; a later cashier never does. They grow more frequent with reputation (the first at level 4, as decision 10 recommends) and with the Gem Shop's planned More Special Customers; a starting knob is about one walk-in in 15. Their faces come from the GDD's cast: knights, ronin, pirates.

Why: salience, a jackpot, and a reason to check the shop and to keep a fine sword on a holder instead of selling it to the next farmer. It gives polish, etch and hilts a job (Alignment findings 3–4), gives reputation levels a reward (finding 7), and keeps a hands-on job that automation never takes. VIPs buy from the shelf; requests stay in the mail. They work from early on, because they're about value, not volume.

### P4. A spare station for the player

Pottery's rule: one more station of each kind than the workers using it. The anvils already follow it (Anvil 1 yours, Anvil 2 the crafter's). Since More refinements the crafter claims the station it's heading to, so with a single furnace it can lock you out of heating. Make **Furnace 2 a prerequisite of Anvil 2**, so one furnace is always free for you. The polishing stone stays shared for now (the crafter holds it 5–10 s a sword); watch whether that bothers anyone.

### P5. Each hire takes one job, and better jobs arrive

Hire order: stocker (Finished Sword Rack → holders), then cashier (regular offers above your threshold, never VIPs), then ore attendant (rolls and fills the Ingot Rack), as Alignment decisions 11–12 recommend. Each removes a routine pull while VIPs, lucky hours, letters and new blueprints add better ones, so the player goes from smith to supplier to manager to the master who serves the special clients. Keep the next hire on screen as the goal, with the coins still needed ([[Design/Alignment Review — 2026-10-23#Worth considering|worth considering 1]]).

Rack sizes set the rhythm. At about 60 s a sword (level 1, estimated from the recorded work times) the crafter fills the Finished Sword Rack in about 6 minutes and empties the Ingot Rack in about 12; at the top level, about 4 and 8. That's how often the player goes to tend it, so bigger racks buy longer stretches of uninterrupted crafting: calm you can buy.

### P6. Sunny spells (rush hour)

The confirmed weather bonus, made noticeable: at random, the sky clears for about 3 minutes, a sun icon with a countdown shows in the HUD, and walk-ins come twice as often. A rush fills the rickshaw bays, so the street visibly gets busy. Only once the crafter works, when there's stock to sell. Weather belongs to the server, so all six shops rush together. Bonus only: other weather never lowers traffic (GDD §7). A timed, announced spell is a step toward the village-wide events the GDD defers; Allen's call.

Why: random timing plus a closing window; stockpiling pays; Better Advertising, More Clients, Rickshaw Bays and extra holders start to matter.

### P7. Lucky hours

At random, about 3 minutes when the loot box rolls better (say one Lucky Roller step up), the box glowing and the odds shown. Starts after Ore Slot 3 or so, and only with somewhere for commons to go: the scrap bin ([[Design/Alignment Review — 2026-10-23#Balance findings|finding 5]]) or more rack space. Otherwise collect-before-reroll and full hands block the roller in the very minutes it matters. It makes Ore Slots 3–6 felt.

### P8. An empty shop that shows (later)

Once the crafter works, customers keep coming on the normal clock; with nothing on display they stop at the door, look and walk on (a shrug, no text). Not earlier: while you make every sword by hand you can't keep stock, and a stream of walk-outs would only show what you can't fix. The tutorial rule stays (no visitors before the first listing); GDD §7 set it for the tutorial, "not yet to all later crafting".

### Also

- **Teach the overlap once:** after the first sword, one guide line: load the next ingot before you forge ([[Design/Core Loop Review#Proposed refinement 3: upgrades should improve a felt limitation|Core Loop refinement 3]]). Don't lengthen heating to imitate the kiln; 10 s already matches a forge.
- **Presence has to pay, and now it does:** since More refinements the cart pays 0.8× against the shop's ≈ 0.9× first offers and ≈ 1.25× patient or liked sales, which is Allen's trade-off ("store means people are going to usually pay more"). Keep it that way: VIPs and rushes should widen the shop's lead for the player who shows up. If the outlet that needs no attention paid as well, the shop's pull would die.

## 5. Guardrails

- **No timers inside the crafts:** the anvil, hilt, polish and etch stay untimed (pillar 2).
- **Nothing owned is lost:** reputation never drops, letters and orders don't expire, and a missed customer leaves the sword on its holder.
- **One surprise at a time:** sunny spells and lucky hours never overlap, with quiet stretches between them.
- **None of it in the first session:** events, VIPs and walk-outs start after Ore Slot 2 at the earliest.
- **No Robux at the moment of urgency:** no "extend the rush" or skip prompts during an event; Robux speeds things up or adds style (GDD §15). About a third of Roblox's age-checked daily users are under 13.
- **Watch stress, not just activity:** more clicking can mean more frustration, not more fun.

## 7. What to watch in the playtest

For [[Design/Alignment Review — 2026-10-23#One playtest session|the playtest session]]:

- After a sale, a ready ingot or the cart's return: does the player go straight to a next job, or stand there?
- How many offers run out while the player is at a station, with today's 20 s.
- How long the crafter stood stopped (empty Ingot Rack or full Finished Sword Rack) before the player noticed.
- Afterwards ask: "What were you hurrying to do?" and "Did you have to leave a sword half-done?"
