# Pottery Tutorial Review

Source: [[References/Create Pottery! game image reference/first 3 minutes of the game.mp4]]. Related: [[Design/Core Loop Review]].

Reviewed the recording visually through sampled frames and closer inspection of tutorial transitions. Duration is **3:44.7**, with timestamps below measured from the recording start. The clip starts with the avatar already beside the clay roller; loading, actual spawn and anything before recording are not shown. Audio was not assessed. This is one recorded session, not evidence of typical new-player speed or measured retention. Desktop notifications in the capture are not game UI.

## Main finding

The opening delivers the game's promise quickly: **get clay → visibly make a bowl → finish it → display it → receive a customer's money**. The first shaping interaction begins around **0:10** and the first sale completes around **1:12**. It teaches through small actions, keeps the player's creation visible, and celebrates a completed economic loop before introducing additional systems.

The later request lesson is less complete than it initially appears. The player accepts a Cocoa Swirl Bowl request but does not deliver it. A **daily reward supplies six gems**, and two of those fund the first Advertisements upgrade. Do not describe this recording as showing a completed commission-to-upgrade loop.

## What it does particularly well

### 1. It lets the player experience the premise almost immediately

The player is shaping clay roughly ten seconds into the recording. The initial instructions are attached to verbs: roll, collect, lower, widen, smooth. The game gives the player a useful object while teaching them. Its strongest opening asset is the transformation itself, supported by the close camera and live feedback.

### 2. Guidance answers both “what?” and “where?”

Short top text names the action; floor chevrons identify the destination; spotlights identify the relevant control. Those signals solve different problems. A player does not have to translate a station name into a search across the plot. The next instruction follows the current activity, rather than presenting the entire workflow at once.

### 3. The first win is achievable and visibly acknowledged

The tutorial approves a 76% bowl. It avoids making the beginner chase perfection before experiencing the business loop. Finish prompts explicitly release the player from a task that otherwise has room for endless adjustment. The sale then combines the visible customer, money, confetti and completion message.

### 4. The same object connects the whole experience

Clay becomes the bowl the player carries, glazes, fires, shelves and sells. Each station changes or advances something already invested in. That continuity gives the transfers a purpose and makes the final sale more meaningful than an unrelated currency grant.

### 5. Additional systems arrive after the player has context

The phone is taught after a completed sale and a second craft. Requests, gems and business upgrades now relate to activities the player has experienced. The initial lesson does not explain every visible upgrade pad, currency and menu.

### 6. Future progress is visible in the world

Green purchase pads sit next to the systems they improve. “Fire Speed $120” is beside the kiln that the player waits for; after the first sale the balance is $105, making the gap understandable. Neighboring shops show fuller, more developed versions of the starter business. These are plausible motivations to continue, not demonstrated retention effects.

### 7. It leaves room to act independently

The second bowl is made without repeating the whole tutorial, and the player skips glazing. Later, the player can walk around while the phone is open. The structure supports moving from guided actions into self-directed play rather than keeping every interaction inside a lesson.

## Gaps and cautions

- **The commission loop is introduced but not completed.** A different gem source advances the upgrade lesson. A robust tutorial should distinguish “you have gems” from “you learned to fulfill a request.” Otherwise it can leave the important middle step unlearned.
- **The first request explicitly asks for rolling until a specific clay lands.** The recording does not reveal its odds or any safeguards. This is the same uncertainty raised for our Iron Wakizashi commission: a specific introductory task can become a material hunt before its reward is understood.
- **Waiting is more noticeable on the second craft.** The player spends much of the kiln wait looking at the kiln. That may be curiosity, but this clip does not demonstrate a compelling parallel task during that wait. Nearby speed upgrades make the benefit legible; they do not by themselves make waiting enjoyable.
- **The guided first craft includes glazing; the second skips it.** The shortcut works, but the clip does not clearly teach the tradeoff between extra finishing, appearance, value and time. Optional work should be an informed choice.
- **The opening success is stronger than the request handoff.** “Tutorial Completed!” arrives at the sale; another guided sequence starts later. Staging lessons is useful, but a persistent chosen goal would help keep the accepted commission visible once the phone closes.
- **One short capture cannot validate long-term retention.** Save/rejoin, D1/D7 return, later crafting variety, mobile usability, monetization pressure and behavior after several sessions are not assessed here.

## Proposed lessons for Sell Swords!

These are recommendations for discussion and testing, not changes to confirmed GDD rules.

1. **Prioritize time to the first hammer interaction and first sale.** My earlier 3–5-minute first-sale target was a planning assumption. This clip's roughly 72-second payoff challenges it. Prototype a compact first-sword route and measure whether new players can reach a satisfying sale in roughly 1–2 minutes; do not force that time by removing the enjoyable forging or treating one recording as a universal benchmark.
2. **Teach the minimum satisfying sword route first.** Roll, heat, shape, assemble, display and sell. Preserve the agreed explicit item pickers, including single-item cases. Introduce optional finishing when its benefit can be understood; do not make every station a prerequisite to the first payoff.
3. **Pair each objective with destination guidance and immediate visual change.** The player should see where to go after each picker and finish action. At the anvil, every taught input should visibly advance the blade.
4. **Complete a dependable teaching commission before claiming that lesson is learned.** Under the existing proposal, supply the first commission's Iron; then show the completed sword being accepted, the promised reward arriving and that reward funding a useful upgrade. Other currency grants should not accidentally skip the delivery lesson.
5. **Make the first upgrade's benefit observable immediately.** For Ore Slot 2, let the player use the new slot and see the extra choice. Do not describe it as doubled income without evidence. Show the next affordable milestone and what it changes.
6. **Support aspiration through visible shops and creations.** A small view of a developed workshop and distinctive swords can explain future possibilities more naturally than an early tour of all progression menus.
7. **Preserve a concrete project for the next session.** A chosen request, next sword shape or workshop goal should remain visible and survive rejoining. A daily reward can supplement that reason to return; this clip does not establish that calendar rewards can replace it.

For a first test, record time to hammer, first sale, independent second sword, commission delivery and first upgrade. Note where players hesitate, how much time is waiting/transfers versus crafting, and whether they can explain what they want to make next. Test desktop and touch separately. Use these observations to refine the opening before interpreting retention numbers.
